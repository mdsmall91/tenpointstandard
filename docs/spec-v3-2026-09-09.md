## The short version

The Field Guide works. Twelve people out of a hundred and sixty two opened it with no distribution pushing them there. The problem is that it asks a cold visitor to do forty questions of homework before it gives them anything.

This spec replaces one long assessment with two lanes at different depths.

**Lane one is The Read.** Ninety seconds. Mostly pictures. No email, no account. It gives a directional position on Align, Design, or Build and names the one thing most likely to stop the project. It is free and it is complete on its own.

**Lane two is The Full Standard.** All ten points, forty questions, scored out of a hundred. It arrives after the person has already received something and already believes the framework is real. The email ask sits here, after value instead of before it.

The reason this works is the same reason Stitch Fix works. Nobody wants to fill out a form about themselves. Everybody will react to a picture. Lane one never asks a person to grade their own project. It shows them things and asks which one is closer to theirs. That takes no effort and it captures a great deal.

Three things carry the design.

**Show, do not ask.** Every question that can be a photograph becomes one. Real Ten Point project photography is the credibility engine and the interaction mechanic at the same time. One tap on a photo captures site type, terrain, and access assumptions together.

**Reward every step.** The current assessment pays out once, at the very end. The new one fills a segment of a ten point ring after each section and writes one line about what it just learned. A person who quits at point six still leaves with six points of value and a reason to come back.

**Answer in words, not a number.** A score out of a hundred feels like a test result. A short written read of their actual project feels like somebody looked at it. That written read is also what gets forwarded to their civil engineer and their lender, which is how one lead becomes three.

The AI does three jobs and is forbidden from a fourth. It reads a free text description and pre-fills what it can. It reads an uploaded photo or site plan and asks smarter follow-ups. It writes the read in the person's own project language. It never produces a number Ten Point would have to defend.

## 2. Site architecture

The Field Journal becomes the front door. A first-time visitor still needs to know what company this is within about three seconds, and a journal index does not say that on its own. A magazine solves this with a masthead, so this site does too.

```
/                        Field Journal index. The homepage.
                         Masthead names Ten Point Services.
/journal/[slug]/         Articles. Each closes with a CTA into The Read.
/read/                   Lane one. The Read. Ninety seconds, ungated.
/standard/               Lane two. The Full Standard. Forty questions.
/about/                  Who Ten Point Services is. One screen.
/consultation/           Talk to a person.
/glamping-show/          Show landing page, QR destination.
```

### 2.1 The masthead

Sits at the top of every page. Always visible, never in the way.

> **The Field Journal**
> Published by Ten Point Services. We are a general contractor. We build campgrounds, RV resorts, glamping properties, and recreation destinations across the United States.

Two lines. That is the entire company overview a visitor needs on this site, because this site is not the company website. The About page carries the longer version for anyone who wants it.

### 2.2 The About page

One screen. No scrolling epic.

- What Ten Point does. General contractor, outdoor hospitality, United States.
- What that covers. Land and entitlements, infrastructure, construction, procurement, opening readiness.
- Three or four real projects with a photograph, the project type, the scope Ten Point held, and one sentence about a real condition solved.
- Where to find the company site.
- A link to The Read.

Do not claim national coverage unless the company can serve a project in any state. If it can, say so plainly and support it in the structured data.

### 2.3 The Journal call to action

Every article gets the same closing block. Same words every time, because repetition is what makes it stick.

> **Where does your project actually stand?**
> Ninety seconds. Ten questions, mostly pictures. No email needed.
> [ See where you are ]

Place it at the end of the article body, and once more inline at roughly the sixty percent scroll mark on articles longer than a thousand words.

## 3. Lane one: The Read

Ten interactions. Target ninety seconds. No email at any point.

Design rule for every screen: the person taps or drags. They never type unless they choose to. Every screen fills the viewport on a phone and works with one thumb.

### Screen 0. The open prompt (optional, skippable)

> **Tell us about your project.**
> A sentence or two is plenty. Skip this if you would rather just tap through.

A single text box and a prominent Skip. Whatever they write goes to the extraction step in section 5. Anything the model can pull with confidence pre-fills a later screen, and that screen is then shown as a confirmation rather than a question.

This is the single biggest time saver in the product. A person who writes "we have 40 acres outside Fredericksburg under contract, want to do about 25 glamping units, hoping to open spring 2028" has just answered five screens.

### Screen 1. Project type

Four photo cards, one tap.

Campground · RV resort · Glamping · Park and recreation

*Feeds:* Property details, Guest experience. Sets the branching path for later screens.

### Screen 2. The land

> **Which of these looks most like your land?**

Four photo cards, one tap.

Wooded · Open ground · On the water · Elevated or rocky

*Feeds:* Property details, Design, Regulatory approvals. Terrain drives access, utilities, and grading assumptions, and a photograph captures it faster than three written questions.

### Screen 3. Where you are today

The most important screen in lane one. A horizontal slider with six stops, each with a small image.

1. Still looking at land
2. Land under contract
3. We own the land
4. Approvals underway
5. Drawings are done
6. Pricing or already building

*Feeds:* Regulatory approvals, Design, and the band calculation. This one interaction replaces roughly six yes or no questions in the current assessment.

### Screen 4. Two-up comparison

> **Which one is closer to what you are building?**

Two real project photographs, side by side. One tap. Run this twice with different pairs.

*Feeds:* Guest experience, Design. This reads ambition and density without asking anyone to grade themselves.

### Screen 5. Size

A slider. The illustration above it changes as the number moves.

> **About how many sites or units?**
> 1 to 200 plus

*Feeds:* Property details, Procurement, Cost certainty.

### Screen 6. How firm is the money

A four stop slider. No dollar figures anywhere.

Still exploring · We have a range · We have a number · The money is committed

*Feeds:* Capital strategy, Cost certainty. People answer a firmness slider honestly and dodge the same question when it is a yes or no.

### Screen 7. How firm is the date

A four stop slider.

No date yet · A season in mind · A target month · A date we have promised somebody

*Feeds:* Schedule, Opening readiness. The last stop is the one that matters. A promised date changes everything about how a project should be run.

### Screen 8. Who is on the team

Multi-select cards. Tap all that apply.

Civil engineer · Architect or designer · Lender · Contractor · Operator · Nobody yet

*Feeds:* Design, Capital strategy, Procurement, Quality assurance. Also the single best predictor of how ready a project really is.

### Screen 9. What worries you

> **What is on your mind the most right now?**

Six cards, one tap.

Whether the land works · Getting approvals · What it will cost · Finding the money · Finding the right builder · Opening on time

*Feeds:* Weights the written read. This is the highest value answer in the whole product for the person who eventually makes the call, because it tells them exactly what to lead with.

### Screen 10. A picture of your project (optional)

> **Have a photo of the land or a site plan? Drop it here.**
> Optional. It helps us give you a better read.

Accepts image or PDF. Handled in section 5.

### Screen 11. The Read

The payoff screen. Four elements, in this order.

**The ring.** Ten segments, one per point. Four or five are lit with a directional read. Five or six are grey and labeled *not yet scored*. The grey is deliberate. It makes the gap visible, and the visible gap is what sells lane two.

**The position.** Align, Design, or Build, with the word *directional* attached. Do not present a number in lane one. A number invites an argument about accuracy that lane one cannot win.

**The written read.** Three or four sentences in plain language, generated from their answers, naming their project back to them and calling out the one gate most likely to stop it. Specification in section 6.

**Two doors, equal weight.**

> **Score all ten points**
> Forty questions. About five minutes. We will email you the full scorecard.
> [ Start the full Standard ]

> **Talk it through with us**
> Twenty minutes with the team that builds these.
> [ Set up a conversation ]

A funded developer will not sit through a quiz. A first-time landowner will not book a call. Both doors have to be open on the same screen, at the same size.

## 4. Lane two: The Full Standard

All ten points. Forty questions. Scored out of a hundred. Email required before the full scorecard is delivered, and the score itself still shows on screen first.

### 4.1 Carry the answers forward

The first screen of lane two is not a question. It is a confirmation.

> **Here is what we already know about your project.**
> A short list of what lane one captured, each with an edit link.
> [ Looks right, keep going ]

Any of the forty questions already answered by lane one is marked answered and skipped. A person who did lane one carefully might face twenty eight questions instead of forty. Say that out loud on this screen, because it is a real reward for having done the first lane.

### 4.2 How a point runs

Each of the ten points runs the same way.

**Open with an image.** A full width real project photograph relevant to that point, with the point name over it.

**One field note.** A single line of plain language from actual project experience. Example shape for Regulatory approvals: *Most projects we see do not lose time at the permit counter. They lose it waiting on a study nobody scheduled.* Kenny writes these. There are ten of them. They are what make this feel like a person rather than a form.

**Four questions, one at a time.** Big tap targets. Three options.

Yes · No · Not sure

*Not sure* scores the same as No and is tracked separately. It is not a failure state, it is the most useful answer in the product. A person with nine *not sure* answers is not unqualified. They are early, and they need a conversation more than a score.

**Fill the ring.** When the fourth question is answered, that segment of the ring fills and one line of result appears immediately.

> **Regulatory approvals: 2 of 4.**
> Approvals are the open gate here. That is normal at your stage.

Do not wait until the end to pay somebody. Pay them ten times.

### 4.3 The result

**The full ring.** All ten segments filled. This is the hero image of the whole product and it should be built to be screenshotted.

**The score and the band.** Out of one hundred. Align, Design, or Build, with the live site's definition of that band.

**The written read.** Longer than lane one. Six to eight sentences. Specification in section 6.

**Three priority actions.** Drawn from the three lowest scoring points, written as things to do rather than things to know.

**The email exchange.**

> **Send me the full scorecard**
> The complete ledger, all forty answers, and your three priority actions. A page you can forward to your engineer, your lender, or your partner.
> Email address
> No spam. Unsubscribe any time.

Then four optional fields, clearly optional: project type, project stage, state, target opening window. A partial answer beats an abandoned form.

**The consultation door.** Same weight as the email card, same as lane one.

**A non-form route.** A phone number and an email address, visible. Some buyers will call, and making them fill out a form to reach a contractor is the wrong move.

### 4.4 The forwardable page

The scorecard that gets emailed is the highest leverage artifact in the system. It is not a receipt. It is a document a person sends to three other people, each of whom then arrives at this site.

It carries the ring, the score, the band, the written read, the full ledger of forty answers, the three priority actions, the Ten Point Services masthead, and one link back to the site. Put a line at the bottom that gives permission to forward it.

> Forward this to your engineer, your lender, or your partner. It is meant to be shared.

## 5. The AI layer

Three jobs. One hard boundary.

### 5.1 Intake extraction

**Input:** the free text from lane one screen 0.
**Output:** a structured object with project type, stage, state, acreage, unit count, timeline, and a confidence value on each field.
**Rule:** only pre-fill a field at high confidence. Show every pre-filled answer as a confirmation the person can change. Never silently assume.

This is the highest leverage AI in the product because it turns typing into skipping.

### 5.2 Image reading

**Input:** the optional photo or site plan from screen 10.
**Output:** observations only. Terrain, apparent vegetation, visible access, water presence, whether the document is a survey, a concept plan, or a construction drawing.
**Rule:** observations feed follow-up questions and the written read. They never feed the score. A photograph is not evidence of a fact about a project.

### 5.3 The written read

**Input:** all answers, the worry from screen 9, and any image observations.
**Output:** plain language prose at an eighth grade reading level. Three to four sentences in lane one, six to eight in lane two.

Rules for the output:

- Describe their project back to them before saying anything about it. People need to see that the system was listening.
- Name the open gate, do not diagnose the cause. Say what the pattern points toward and suggest looking into it.
- Never negative about the person. A gap is a gap, not a mistake.
- No em dashes. One fact per sentence. Full conversational sentences.
- Never invent a fact they did not give.

### 5.4 What the AI must never produce

This list is not a preference. It is a boundary for a licensed general contractor.

- **No cost estimates.** Not a range, not an order of magnitude, not a per site number. Cost certainty is a paid service and a wrong number is a real problem.
- **No schedule projections.** No opening dates, no duration estimates.
- **No yield, revenue, or return modeling.** That is a feasibility study and this product explicitly is not one.
- **No code, permit, or regulatory rulings.** Never tell someone what their county will approve.
- **No open construction chatbot.** It creates liability and it gives away the offer.

### 5.5 Build guardrails

- Calls go through a server side proxy. A Cloudflare Worker or equivalent. No API key ever reaches client code.
- Constrain the model output to a schema. Reject and retry anything off schema.
- Every generated read has a template fallback keyed to band and worry. If the call fails or times out, the person still gets a result. The product must never depend on a model being up.
- Rate limit the proxy per session.
- Kenny reviews a sample of generated reads across all three bands before launch. Nothing ships in Ten Point's voice unread.

## 6. Measurement

The current property has no completion event, which means nobody can tell whether any of this worked. Ship the events with the build, not after it.

**Lane one**

`read_start`, `read_screen_complete` with `screen_number`, `read_abandon` with `last_screen`, `read_complete` with `band` and `worry`, `read_intake_used`, `read_image_uploaded`.

**Lane two**

`standard_start` with `carried_from_read`, `standard_point_complete` with `point_number`, `point_name`, `point_score`, `standard_complete` with `score`, `band`, `not_sure_count`, `questions_skipped_by_carry`, `standard_abandon` with `last_point`.

**Conversion**

`scorecard_email_submitted`, `consultation_cta_clicked` with `placement`, `consultation_form_submitted`, `scorecard_forwarded` if a share action exists.

**Key events**

`read_complete`, `standard_complete`, `scorecard_email_submitted`, `consultation_form_submitted`, `journal_subscribe`.

**The funnel to build**

`session_start` to `read_start` to `read_complete` to `standard_start` to `standard_complete` to `scorecard_email_submitted`.

**Guard every event so it fires once.** The current property shows nine `fg_email_captured` events from three users. Every count in it is inflated by an unknown factor. Do not carry that forward.

**Send no personal information to analytics.** No emails, no names, no phone numbers.

## 7. What to watch after launch

Four numbers answer four different questions.

| Number | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Lane one completion rate | Whether ninety seconds is short enough. Target above 60%. |
| Lane one to lane two rate | Whether the grey ring is doing its job. Target above 25%. |
| Lane two completion rate | Whether forty questions is the barrier everyone assumes it is. |
| Not sure count per assessment | Where the buyer actually is. A high count is a conversation, not a bad lead. |

If lane one completes well and almost nobody moves to lane two, the problem is the exchange on the result screen, not the assessment. If lane two starts well and dies at point four, the problem is a specific point. Right now neither question can be answered, which is why the events ship with the build.

## A4. Copy rules for everything in the product

- Eighth grade reading level throughout. If an eighth grader cannot follow it, it is wrong.
- No em dashes. Use a period, a comma, or a second sentence.
- One fact per sentence. Never stack two or three data points into one line.
- Full conversational sentences. No clipped analyst fragments.
- Never negative about the person or their project. Frame a gap as something the data points toward and worth looking into.
- No industry jargon and no technology jargon. Nobody outside this building needs to know how the read is generated.

## A5. Accessibility

- Every photo choice card has a visible text label. The image is not the only signal.
- Score and state are never communicated by color alone. The ring uses fill and label together.
- Tap targets at least 44 by 44 pixels.
- Sliders operable by keyboard, with accessible names.
- The whole flow works with one thumb on a phone. That is the booth tablet case and the LinkedIn case at the same time.
